How Long Does a Sump Pump Install Take in Marblehead, MA?

A pump swap is an hour or two. A new pit with a discharge line is a one to two day project. Here is what sets the timeline and what Marblehead's rules add.

Swap, new pit or new system

How long a sump pump install takes depends on what is already there. Replacing a pump in an existing pit is quick. Digging a new pit in a slab is not, and adding a battery backup or a new discharge route adds more time.

The honest answer from a plumber is to ask what exists now. The three situations below cover most basements.

Replacing a pump in an existing pit

This is the quick job. The plumber disconnects the old pump, checks the pit, installs the new pump with a new check valve and tests it. One to two hours is normal, plus time to check the discharge line outside.

Replacing the check valve and float at the same time prevents the most common repeat failures.

Installing a new pit

A new pit means cutting the slab, digging out gravel, setting a liner and pouring a collar around it. Cutting concrete is loud and dusty, and the slab needs time to cure before the floor is finished. Plan for one to two days, depending on the slab thickness.

In Marblehead, ledge can sit close to the surface, and a pit may meet rock. The plumber checks the site first, since hitting ledge changes the plan.

Local note

Local note: where a Marblehead sump pump may discharge

According to Marblehead's by-laws, discharging stormwater, groundwater and similar water into the sanitary sewer is barred, and the town's Sewer Department runs a program to find and remove sump pumps wrongly connected to it. A homeowner who wants to connect to the town drainage system applies for a license agreement for a private connection, which the Department of Public Works describes as carrying a $25 fee and requiring inspection of the completed connection.

If you do not connect to town drainage, the town says pumped water must not create a hazard on public ways, including ice. Plan the discharge before the pump goes in, and ask the Department of Public Works what applies at your address.

Follow the pump maker's guidelines for pit size, float clearance and check valve placement, since a pump that cannot swing its float freely will fail early.

Adding backup and discharge work

A battery backup pump needs its own float and a spot for the battery, and it adds a couple of hours. A water-powered backup needs a plumbing connection and a drain path. New discharge piping to the outdoors or to a legal connection can add half a day or more.

A pump with no backup stops in a power outage, which is when it is most needed.

What can slow the job down

Ledge or rock

Hard digging adds time.

Finished basement

Walls and flooring need protection or removal.

Long discharge route

More piping and trenching.

Permit and inspection

Scheduling an inspection adds days.

What to do after the install: test and maintain

Pour a bucket of water into the pit and watch the pump start, empty the pit and shut off. Listen for a check valve that bangs or a pump that cycles repeatedly. Test it a few times a year and before storm season. See sump pump repair if it fails.

If the basement stays wet despite a working pump, the cause may be drainage, and basement waterproofing covers that.

How to prepare the basement

Clear a path to the pit and move stored items away from the work area. Cutting a slab throws dust, so cover finished items and ask the crew about dust control. Keep a clear route for the wet vacuum and the discharge line.

If a storm is forecast, tell the plumber. A pump that is failing before a storm may be swapped quickly, and a bigger project can wait until the weather clears.

Keeping the pump reliable after the install

Test the pump each spring and fall, clean the intake screen and check the discharge outlet outdoors for ice, leaves or a collapsed hose. Replace the battery in a backup every few years, even if it seems fine.

Keep a record of the install date, the model and the service dates. When the pump is eight to ten years old, plan a replacement before the next big storm.
